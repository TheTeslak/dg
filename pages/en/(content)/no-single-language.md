Accessibility as a design principle is close to my heart. In this blog, I thought through keyboard navigation and screen reader support for blind users

[I articulated](https://t.me/TesNot/7) a similar thought about duplicating content when starting my Telegram blog

Going multilingual extends the same principle

Translating into every language is hard. I picked the popular ones and those where people I know can point out nuances:

- Russian as my native language
- English as the international language
- [Spanish]{term="Spanish" definition="Español neutro — a version without regional bias, understood from Spain to Argentina"} and Portuguese, which I am picking up little by little
- French, which I had in school
- and German!

+ it's just interesting to see the differences

[I know absolutely nothing about Asian languages. After studying the nuances of CJK, I decided not to risk translating without an editor. Future generations of neural networks will probably make it possible to translate into them too — after all, I want the whole world]{.muted}

Built-in browser translators are a bit weak, and without a translation, a text has a hard time crossing the boundaries of its language bubble

If no translation is available, the site will show the publication in an available language

![Original characters (Teslaks) wearing traditional costumes from different countries|no-caption](/images/multilang.avif)

## Translation methodology

I put a lot of time into the original and aim for comparable quality in translation

A long prompt translates the text in chunks and explains the differences between phrasing options. I read closely and choose, then pass it to another AI — and so on in a loop, minimizing inaccuracies

Facts and names are localized [in not-yet-published articles]

Drafts are mostly in Russian, so I translate them. Though I am writing a new article on design in English

I edit the English version myself; the rest get less attention because I don't know them well enough
