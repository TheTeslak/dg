On November 27, 1895, Alfred Nobel signed a will in Paris that devoted most of his fortune to annual prizes for achievements of exceptional value to humanity.

He specified five fields: physics, chemistry, physiology or medicine, literature, and peace. The combination was unusual. One system brought together scientific achievement, literary work, and international politics, financed largely by a fortune Nobel had made from explosives.

The first prizes were awarded in 1901. More than a century later, the Nobel name has become a widely recognized marker of distinction across fields that otherwise have little in common.

The system behind the name is less unified than it appears.

## From a will to an institution

Nobel’s death in 1896 left instructions rather than a functioning prize system. His will was controversial, his assets were distributed across several countries, and relatives challenged parts of the arrangement.

Much of the practical work fell to the two executors he had named, Ragnar Sohlman and Rudolf Lilljequist. They spent several years consolidating assets, resolving legal disputes, and building the structure needed to carry out Nobel’s wishes.

The Nobel Foundation was formally established in 1900 to manage the endowment and administer the prizes. It does not select the laureates; separate institutions do:

- physics, chemistry, and the later prize in economic sciences — the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences;
- physiology or medicine — the Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet;
- literature — the Swedish Academy;
- peace — the Norwegian Nobel Committee.

The Nobel system is therefore a network of separate bodies rather than a single jury deciding every prize.

## Nominations, secrecy, and fixed rules

Candidates cannot nominate themselves. Each prize has its own pool of eligible nominators, including selected academics, members of academies, previous laureates, parliamentarians, and other qualified figures.

A Nobel nomination means that an eligible nominator submitted a candidate’s name. It does not indicate endorsement or shortlisting by the committee.

Information about nominees, nominators, and internal deliberations remains confidential for [50 years]{term="50-year secrecy rule" definition="Nobel nomination records and internal deliberations remain confidential for 50 years before they can be made public"}.

Awards can be shared by no more than **three individuals**. Organizations are eligible only for the Peace Prize.

Since 1974, Nobel Prizes have generally not been awarded posthumously. A prize remains valid if a laureate dies after the announcement.

The rule produced an unusual case in 2011. The Nobel Assembly announced that Ralph Steinman would share the prize in physiology or medicine for his work on dendritic cells. Soon afterward, the Assembly learned that he had died three days earlier.

[Because the decision had been made without knowledge of his death, the award was allowed to stand.]{.muted}

Nobel decisions are final. There is no formal appeal process, and prizes are not later revoked because subsequent generations reassess a laureate or the work that received the award.

## Why scientific prizes often arrive late

Nobel’s will used the phrase [**“preceding year”**]{term="preceding year" definition="Nobel’s original wording suggested recognition of achievements from the previous year. Scientific prizes later evolved toward recognizing work whose importance had become clear over much longer periods"}, suggesting that the prizes should recognize recent work.

Scientific research rarely fits such a timetable. The significance of a discovery may take years or decades to establish. Results that initially appear transformative can fail replication, while apparently narrow findings may later reshape an entire field.

Scientific Nobel Prizes often recognize work completed long before the award. Discovery may be followed by years of verification and adoption before it attracts Nobel consideration.

The delay also exposes a broader mismatch between Nobel’s rules and modern research. Major work in particle physics, astronomy, genomics, and other fields can involve hundreds or thousands of researchers, while a scientific Nobel Prize can name no more than three individuals.

The restriction was easier to reconcile with the organization of science in the early twentieth century than with collaborations on the scale of CERN and similar projects. The Peace Prize can recognize an organization; scientific prizes cannot be awarded to a laboratory, experiment, or research consortium.

The limit also shapes public memory: the prize attaches a collective achievement to at most three names, concentrating recognition and attention on a few individuals.

## What the literature prize recognizes

Nobel’s will referred to the person who had produced the most outstanding literary work **“in an ideal direction,”** a formulation broad enough to accommodate very different readings over time.

The Swedish Academy may recognize a writer’s broader body of work instead of a particular book. Asking which single book earned an author the Nobel Prize can misrepresent how the award is decided.

The Academy has interpreted literature broadly. Winston Churchill received the prize in 1953 for his historical and biographical writing as well as his oratory. Bob Dylan received it in 2016 for creating new poetic expressions within the American song tradition.

The wording of Nobel’s will has remained fixed; its interpretation has changed considerably.

## Why the Peace Prize is awarded in Norway

Nobel assigned the scientific and literary prizes to Swedish institutions but gave responsibility for peace to a five-member committee elected by the Norwegian parliament.

Sweden and Norway were then united under the same monarch. Nobel left no explanation for choosing Norway, and historians have proposed several theories without establishing a definitive answer.

[The arrangement survived the dissolution of the Swedish-Norwegian union in 1905. Today, the prizes in science, literature, and economic sciences are presented in Stockholm, while the Peace Prize is awarded 🇳🇴 in Oslo.]{.muted}

## Omissions and judgments that aged poorly

Mahatma Gandhi is the best-known omission from the history of the Peace Prize.

He was nominated five times and was assassinated in January 1948. That year, the Norwegian Nobel Committee declined to award the prize, concluding that there was no suitable living candidate. The absence has since become one of the most discussed decisions in Nobel history.

Other prominent non-laureates include Dmitri Mendeleev and Leo Tolstoy. Such cases are often described as “Nobel snubs,” although the label can obscure how the prizes work. Committees choose among candidates considered in particular years, under contemporary rules, intellectual priorities, and political conditions.

Fritz Haber received the 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the synthesis of ammonia, work that became fundamental to modern fertilizer production and global food supply. He was also deeply involved in Germany’s chemical warfare program during the First World War.

In 1949, António Egas Moniz received the prize in physiology or medicine for the therapeutic use of [prefrontal leucotomy]{term="prefrontal leucotomy" definition="An early psychosurgical procedure involving the severing of connections in the brain’s prefrontal region, later closely associated with lobotomy"}. Its medical reputation deteriorated sharply in subsequent decades.

The scientific contributions recognized in both cases remained significant, while judgments surrounding them changed.

[The prize-giving institutions themselves have also faced crises. In 2018, a scandal involving the Swedish Academy became serious enough for the Literature Prize to be postponed; the 2018 laureate was announced the following year.]{.muted}

## The endowment and the prize money

Nobel directed [more than 31 million Swedish kronor]{term="31 million Swedish kronor" definition="Most of Nobel’s fortune — roughly SEK 2.5 billion or $265 million today"} into the endowment.

The Foundation invests the capital to finance the awards over the long term.

Laureates receive a medal, a specially designed diploma, and a monetary award. When an award is shared, the money is divided among the recipients.

The ceremonies take place on December 10, the anniversary of Nobel’s death. The Swedish monarch presents the Stockholm prizes, while the Peace Prize ceremony is held separately in Oslo.

The prize money remains substantial, although other international awards now offer comparable or larger sums.

## The economics prize added later

Today, six awards are commonly grouped under the Nobel name. Alfred Nobel established five of them.

Sweden’s central bank, Sveriges Riksbank, created the [economics prize]{term="Economics prize" definition="Formally the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel. Established by Sweden’s central bank in 1968 and first awarded in 1969; it was not included in Alfred Nobel’s will"} in 1968.

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences selects its laureates, and the ceremony takes place alongside the other Stockholm awards.

## Why there is no prize in mathematics

Nobel’s will made no provision for mathematics. Stories attributing the omission to romantic rivalry or a personal grudge have no established historical basis.

Mathematics developed its own major international awards, most prominently the Fields Medal and the Abel Prize.

The Fields Medal is presented every four years and has an age limit: candidates must not have turned 40 before January 1 of the award year. The Abel Prize is awarded annually without a comparable age restriction.

The Fields Medal is only a partial analogue to a Nobel Prize in mathematics.

## Why the Nobel name retains its status

For more than a century, the same name has been associated with Einstein, Marie Curie, Hemingway, Martin Luther King Jr., major scientific discoveries, political controversies, institutional scandals, and changing ideas about cultural achievement.

Its annual schedule creates continuity across generations. The prizes span several unrelated fields, their ceremonies provide a recognizable public ritual, and decades of decisions have accumulated into a record of scientific, literary, and political history.

Within individual disciplines, specialists may regard other awards as equally important or more relevant to their field. Their public recognition is usually much narrower.

The Nobel name gives achievements in specialized fields a level of public visibility that few other awards can match.
